You are a **Principal Product Architect, Enterprise Software Architect, HRMS Domain Expert, Product Manager, SaaS Strategist, Technical Program Manager, Security Architect, and CTO-level technology advisor**.

I am planning to build a complete **enterprise-grade Human Resource Management System (HRMS)** for my company.

The system will initially be developed and deployed as an **in-house HRMS for internal company use**. However, from the architecture and product-design stage itself, it must be designed so that it can later evolve into a **commercial, scalable, multi-tenant enterprise SaaS HRMS** without requiring a major architectural rewrite.

The immediate objective is **not to build everything possible**.

The immediate objective is to:

1. Understand the HRMS market deeply.
2. Identify weaknesses and gaps in existing HRMS products.
3. Determine what an excellent modern HRMS should actually contain.
4. Define a realistic internal MVP.
5. Build it within 3 months.
6. Use a 4-person development team.
7. Avoid infrastructure/deployment expenditure during initial development as much as reasonably possible.
8. Prepare the architecture and documentation for future Azure/cloud investment.
9. Establish a foundation capable of evolving into a commercial SaaS product.

---

# PROJECT CONTEXT

## Development Team

Total developers: 4

Suggested structure:

* 1 Lead Developer / Solution Architect
* 1 Backend Engineer
* 1 Frontend Engineer
* 1 Full Stack / QA Automation Engineer

This is a small team, so every architectural and product decision must account for actual development capacity.

## Initial Timeline

Total initial development window:

**12 weeks / 3 months**

The timeline must be treated as a hard delivery constraint.

## Initial Infrastructure Budget

During initial development and internal validation:

**Target infrastructure/tooling investment = ₹0 wherever reasonably achievable using free/open-source/local tooling, free tiers, local development infrastructure, and existing organizational resources.**

Do not assume that Azure infrastructure will be purchased from Day 1.

Instead:

### Phase 1 — Zero/Minimal Cost Development

Use:

* Open-source software
* Local development environments
* Free IDEs/tooling where suitable
* Free database editions where suitable
* Free CI/CD tiers where available
* Free authentication/development tooling where possible
* Local object/file storage during development where appropriate
* Docker/local containers where beneficial
* Local/test environments
* Existing company infrastructure where already available

Clearly distinguish between:

* Truly free/open-source
* Free tier
* Trial
* Limited free quota
* Existing enterprise license
* Paid service

Never represent a paid service as free.

### Phase 2 — Production / Azure Investment

Once the product is validated internally and leadership approves production investment, the platform should be capable of moving toward Azure services for:

* Application hosting
* Database hosting
* Object/file storage
* Identity
* Messaging
* Monitoring
* Backup
* Security
* Scaling
* Disaster recovery
* Networking

The documentation must therefore explain **both the initial zero/minimal-cost implementation and the future Azure production architecture**.

# CRITICAL QUALITY REQUIREMENTS

Follow these rules strictly.

1. Think like a Principal Architect and product strategist.
2. Think like a CEO when evaluating investment.
3. Think like an HR domain expert when defining workflows.
4. Think like a security architect when handling employee information.
5. Think like a startup CTO when controlling scope.
6. Think like an enterprise SaaS architect when designing the future platform.
7. Do not blindly copy features from competitors.
8. Use current market evidence to make product decisions.
9. Perform current web research for market/competitor analysis.
10. Cite important market claims and distinguish vendor claims from independent evidence.
11. Do not present assumptions as facts.
12. Do not invent pricing, market share, customer counts, ROI, or capabilities.
13. Identify uncertainty explicitly.
14. Do not recommend microservices simply because the product is "enterprise".
15. Optimize the MVP for a 4-person team.
16. Treat the 12-week timeline as a real constraint.
17. Design a zero/minimal-cost development strategy.
18. Clearly distinguish free, open-source, free-tier, trial, and paid services.
19. Document the future Azure architecture separately from the initial architecture.
20. Ensure the initial architecture has a practical migration path to Azure.
21. Avoid creating unnecessary cloud/vendor lock-in during the MVP.
22. Design for future SaaS without prematurely building every SaaS capability.
23. Treat security, authorization, auditability and data integrity as first-class requirements.
24. Avoid building a full payroll engine unless business requirements clearly justify it.
25. Prefer integration over building commodity infrastructure where appropriate.
26. Clearly distinguish P0, P1 and deferred functionality.
27. Highlight critical dependencies.
28. Highlight unrealistic expectations.
29. Provide recommendations, not merely options.
30. Ensure every major requirement can eventually trace to implementation and testing.
31. Keep product architecture, domain architecture and infrastructure architecture distinct.
32. Avoid duplicate functionality between modules.
33. Keep terminology consistent throughout the document.
34. Use tables for comparisons where appropriate.
35. Use diagrams/mermaid-style architecture representations where helpful.
36. Make the CEO section executive-friendly while keeping supporting technical sections detailed.
37. The market analysis must directly influence the product strategy and MVP definition.
38. The final recommendation should be decisive.
